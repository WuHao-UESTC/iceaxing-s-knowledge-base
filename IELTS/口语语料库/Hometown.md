# 二、句式模板

## 2.1 Part 1 适用句式

**介绍家乡（农村 + 离乡背景）**
- Technically I'm from a small village in Xuanhan County, which is under Dazhou City in Sichuan. But to be honest, I don't have a huge amount of memories there because my parents moved to Guangdong for work when I was very young.
- I grew up mostly away from my hometown — my parents were migrant workers in Guangdong, so I only really got to know the village later in life.

**表达对家乡的感受（疏离但眷恋）**
- I have mixed feelings about it, to be honest. I'm not deeply attached to it the way some people are, but at the same time, going back once a year does feel grounding in a weird way.
- I wouldn't say I have a strong emotional connection to it — I've spent too little time there. But there's something about it that sticks with you. The quiet, the smells, the way people live — it's hard to put into words.

**描述赶集日的反差**
- The thing that has always stuck with me is market day. Most of the time the village is dead quiet — you could hear a pin drop. But on market days, it's like someone flipped a switch. Suddenly there are people everywhere, stalls lining the streets, the whole place buzzing.
- What really stands out in my memory is the contrast. Day to day, nothing happens. But when the market comes to town, the place absolutely comes alive.

**描述菜市场场景**
- The food market is the heart of it all — noisy, a bit chaotic, but incredibly vibrant. The stalls are piled high with everything you can imagine, and the air is thick with the smell of cooking and fresh produce.
- I remember the ground was always a bit of a mess — muddy if it had rained, bits of vegetable leaves everywhere. It wasn't clean or fancy, but it felt real, you know? Unpolished, but genuine.

**谈论与老一辈的距离**
- I have huge respect for the older generation back home, even though I can't fully relate to how they live. They're up before dawn every day working the fields — it's a completely different world from mine.
- I won't pretend I understand their lifestyle — I don't. I'm used to city life now. But I admire them immensely. Their hard work and, I suppose, their stubbornness, are the reason I had the chance to leave.

## 2.2 Part 2 适用句式

**开头引入（承认记忆稀疏）**
- I'm going to talk about my home village, which is tucked away in the hills of Xuanhan County in Sichuan. I should say upfront — I don't have as many memories there as most people do of their hometowns, because my parents moved away for work when I was very young.
- The place I want to describe is the village where my family is from. Now, I live in Chengdu and I only make it back about once a year, so my connection to it is a bit different from most people's.

**描述日常的宁静**
- On a normal day, it's incredibly quiet — almost eerily so. You could hear a pin drop. There's no traffic, no shops blasting music, just the occasional dog barking or the sound of someone working in the distance.
- The pace of life is glacial compared to the city. People aren't in a rush — there's nowhere to rush to. The old folks sit outside their houses in the late afternoon, just watching the world go by.

**描述赶集日 + 菜市场（画面感）**
- But then market day rolls around and it's a completely different story. The main street, which is usually empty, suddenly fills up with stalls — vegetables, live chickens, homemade tofu, you name it. Vendors are calling out their prices, and the air is thick with the smell of sizzling oil, fresh herbs, steamed buns — it's an assault on the senses in the best possible way.
- The busiest spot by far is the food market. Stalls overflowing with produce, people haggling, kids running around. The ground is often muddy — especially if it's rained — and it's not what you'd call clean. But somehow, the messiness is part of its charm. It's unpolished and real, not some sanitised supermarket.

**描述老一辈的生活方式**
- One thing that really stays with me is the older generation — people like my grandparents and their neighbours. They have this rhythm to their lives that hasn't changed in, I don't know, maybe centuries. They're up at four in the morning, before the sun gets too brutal, heading up to the hills to work their plots of land. They come back around midday, cook lunch, and that's basically their day.
- They've worked those same few acres their entire lives. And here's the thing — they never seem to complain. I live a completely different life now, in a city, with screens and schedules and deadlines, and I honestly can't wrap my head around their way of living. But I've come to realise that their sacrifice, their sheer stubbornness in sticking with that life, is what gave me the chance to have a different one. So even though I don't fully understand them, I feel this deep sense of gratitude.

**收尾（疏离 + 感激的矛盾情感）**
- So yeah, that's my hometown — or home village, more accurately. I go back once a year, and each time I feel a bit like an outsider, to be honest. But I also feel grounded. It reminds me where I came from, and what was given up for me to leave. It's complicated, but I think that's what makes it meaningful.
- All in all, it's not the kind of place I'd choose to live, but it's the kind of place I'll always carry with me. If that makes any sense.

## 2.3 Part 3 适用句式

**农村 vs 城市生活**
- I think it comes down to what you value. The countryside offers peace, space, and a sense of rootedness that cities just can't replicate. But cities offer opportunity, convenience, and a kind of freedom that rural life doesn't. I don't think one is objectively better — it really depends on the person and the stage of life they're in.
- Having experienced both, I'd say the trade-offs are pretty stark. Village life is simpler and in some ways more authentic, but it can also feel limiting. City life is exciting and full of possibility, but it can be exhausting and a bit soulless at times.

**代际差异 / 老一辈的生活方式是否会消失**
- Realistically, I think it is a dying way of life. My generation, for the most part, isn't going back. We've tasted a different world — education, cities, career paths — and the idea of waking up at four to work the fields just doesn't fit anymore. In a generation or two, I suspect villages like mine will look very different, or they might not exist at all in the way they do now.
- I have mixed feelings about it. On one hand, you don't want to romanticise poverty or backbreaking labour — there's a reason people leave. But on the other hand, there's knowledge and values embedded in that way of life that will be lost when it disappears. It's hard to know how to feel.

**如何看待离开家乡的年轻人**
- I think it's natural and almost inevitable. Young people want more than what a small village can offer — more choices, more experiences, more room to define their own lives. I can relate to that personally; I made the same choice. But I think it comes at a cost — the ties to home get thinner with each generation.
- I don't think leaving is a betrayal or anything like that. But I do think it's worth staying connected in whatever way you can — even if it's just going back once a year, even if you feel a bit out of place. Knowing where you're from matters, even if you're not going back to stay.

# 三、文化背景 & 中式英语陷阱

## 3.1 常见误区

| ❌ 中式表达 | ✅ 地道表达 | 为什么 |
|------------|------------|--------|
| My hometown is a beautiful village. | It's not much to look at, but it's got its own charm. / It's a pretty rustic kind of place. | 英语中描述农村不用 "beautiful"（听起来像旅游广告）。用 rustic / charming in its own way / got character 更真实 |
| The village is very quiet. | Most days, you could hear a pin drop. / The place is dead quiet. | "very quiet" 太直白。英语口语中善用画面感的夸张说法 |
| The old people are very hard-working. | The older generation are a different breed — they're up at the crack of dawn and never seem to complain. | 不要用 "hard-working" 这种标签式形容，用具体行为来 show, don't tell |
| The market is very lively. | The market absolutely comes alive — it's noisy, a bit chaotic, but the energy is incredible. | "lively" 本身没错但弱了。come alive 是英语中描述场所从静到动的核心短语 |
| I can't understand them. (指老一辈) | I can't fully relate to their way of life. / I struggle to make sense of how they live. | "understand" 可能显得居高临下。can't relate / struggle to make sense of 更温和 |
| I respect them. (太简短) | I have huge respect for them. / I admire them more than I can really express. / I owe them a great deal. | 这是一个情绪高潮点，英语中需要有重量感的表达，不能轻飘飘的一个 respect 就过了 |

## 3.2 农村话题特殊词汇对照

| 中文概念 | ❌ 直译 | ✅ 地道说法 |
|---------|---------|-----------|

| 烟火气 | fire smoke air (✗) | the buzz / the earthy, lived-in atmosphere / the smell of cooking and chatter of people / it feels alive |

## 3.3 英美文化背景

**英国人如何谈论乡村**
- 英国人对 countryside 有很深的文化情结（pastoral ideal），但他们描述乡村同样倾向于 understatement："It's a bit out of the way"（地方有点偏）实际上可能是 "in the middle of nowhere"
- "the back of beyond" 是英式表达，意思是非常非常偏远，雅思用很加分
- "a different breed" 描述某一类人时在英式口语中很自然，没有贬义

**美国人对 rural life 的表述**
- 美国人说 rural area / the country / small-town life，中西部农业州有强烈的 farming identity
- "a dying way of life" 在美国语境中常用来谈论小农场被大农业企业取代，雅思中可以直接套用
- 美式口语中 "I couldn't hack it"（我吃不消）表达自己无法适应某种生活的诚实态度

**谈论代际牺牲时的文化分寸**
- 英语中对家人的牺牲用 **understatement + 重量词** 的组合。比如 "I owe them a great deal" 比 "I'm so grateful" 更有分量
- "paved the way" 字面是铺路，在英语中是表达 "为后人创造了机会" 的最核心短语
- 不要在口语中过于 melodramatic（戏剧化），保持平静克制反而更能打动人——这恰好符合英式表达传统

## 3.4 语用注意事项

- **记忆稀疏不是劣势**：你说 "I don't have many memories" 完全 OK，考官不是在测试你的记忆力。关键是你如何描述这种 **有限但深刻的连接**。诚实 + 细节 远超虚构的"完美家乡"
- **市场场景的五感描写**：雅思高分口语的标志之一是 **sensory detail**——气味（smell of sizzling oil）、声音（vendors calling out）、触感（muddy underfoot）、视觉（stalls piled high）。你提供的素材恰好能支撑这种描写
- **矛盾情感比单一赞美更真实**："I feel detached but grateful" 这种 nuanced 的表达在 Part 3 中非常加分，因为这展示了你能处理复杂情感的语言能力
- **地名说明**：Xuanhan County, Dazhou City, Sichuan Province——考官不需要知道在哪儿，但给出定位链条会显得回答完整。可以简化为 "a village in the hills of Sichuan"
- **避免政治敏感表述**：不要直接说 "China's migrant worker problem" 之类。用中性叙事：parents moved away for work / I was raised elsewhere / this is a common story

# 四、实战语料

## 4.1 Part 1 模拟问答

**Q:** Where are you from?
**A:** Technically, I'm from a small village in Xuanhan County, which is in the hills of eastern Sichuan. But to be honest, I don't have a ton of memories from growing up there — my parents moved to Guangdong for work when I was very young, so I spent most of my childhood elsewhere. These days I live in Chengdu, and I go back to the village about once a year. So my connection to it is... well, it's there, but it's not as deep as it would be for someone who grew up surrounded by it.

**Q:** What do you like about your hometown?
**A:** What stands out most in my memory is market day — when the village comes alive. Most of the time the place is so quiet you could hear a pin drop. But on market days, the main street fills up with stalls, and the whole atmosphere shifts. The smell of food cooking, vendors calling out, the market piled high with produce — it's chaotic, but there's something really comforting about it. It's not polished or fancy at all — the ground's often muddy, things are a bit messy — but that's exactly what makes it feel genuine. It's got this earthy, lived-in feel that I've never found in a city.

**Q:** Has your hometown changed much over the years?
**A:** To be honest, not as much as you might expect — and I think that's partly because it's quite remote. Some of the younger people have moved away to cities, so the village feels a bit emptier than it used to be. There might be a few more modern things — better roads, more people with phones, that kind of thing. But the rhythm of life? The way the older generation live? That hasn't really changed. They're still up at dawn working the fields, and market day is still the highlight of the week. It's almost like the village is in a little bubble, holding onto something the rest of the world is letting go of.

**Q:** Would you want to live there in the future?
**A:** Honestly? Probably not. I've gotten used to city life — the convenience, the opportunities, the anonymity even. Going back once a year is nice, and it does feel grounding, but living there full-time? I think I'd struggle. My life is just too different now. Having said that, I wouldn't want to lose the connection entirely. It's important to me to keep going back, even if I feel a bit like an outsider these days. There's something about knowing where you're from that matters, even if you're not going back to stay.

## 4.2 Part 2 模拟独白

**Cue Card:**
> Describe your hometown.
> You should say:
> - Where it is
> - What it is like
> - What there is to see and do
> - And explain how you feel about it

**Sample Answer:**

I'm going to talk about my home village, which is tucked away in the hills of Xuanhan County in eastern Sichuan. I should say right at the start — I don't have as many memories of it as most people do of their hometowns, because my parents moved to Guangdong for work when I was very young. I live in Chengdu now and I only make it back about once a year. So my perspective is maybe a bit different — I'm kind of half insider, half outsider.

In terms of what it's like — most days, it's incredibly quiet. I mean, you could hear a pin drop. No traffic, no loud music, just the occasional dog barking or someone working in the distance. The summers are scorching hot — like walking into a wall of heat — but the winters are surprisingly mild. You don't even need a thick coat. Life moves slowly. The older folks sit outside their houses in the late afternoon, just watching the day go by. It's the kind of stillness you never get in a city.

But then there's market day, and I have to say, that's where my strongest memories live. The main street — usually empty — suddenly fills up with people and stalls. You hear vendors calling out their prices, and the air is thick with the smell of sizzling oil, fresh herbs, steamed buns. The food market is the heart of it — stalls overflowing with vegetables, tofu, live chickens, you name it. The ground is often muddy, especially if it's rained, and it's not what anyone would call clean or tidy. But that's the thing — it's not supposed to be. It's unpolished and authentic. There's something about that earthy, messy, bustling atmosphere that I find incredibly grounding.

What I think about most, though, isn't the place itself — it's the people, especially the older generation like my grandparents and their neighbours. They live by this rhythm that hasn't changed in decades. They're up at four in the morning, before the sun gets too harsh, and they head up to the hills to work their plots of land. They come back around midday, have lunch, and that's their day. They've worked the same few acres their entire lives, and they never seem to complain. Honestly, I can't wrap my head around it. My life — with screens and schedules and deadlines — is a completely different world.

And I think that's the heart of how I feel about my hometown. I feel a bit detached from it, if I'm honest. I've gotten used to the city and I don't think I could go back to that way of life. But at the same time, I feel this deep sense of gratitude. Their stubborn hard work — getting up before dawn day after day, year after year — is what paved the way for me to leave, to get an education, to build a different kind of life. I don't fully understand them, and maybe I never will. But I respect them immensely, and going back once a year, even if I feel a bit out of place, reminds me of that. It's complicated — but I think that's what makes it real.

## 4.3 Part 3 模拟讨论

**Q:** Do you think rural life and urban life attract different kinds of people?
**A:** Definitely. I think they suit different personalities, or maybe different stages of life. Rural life attracts people who value peace, routine, and a strong sense of place — people who don't mind that nothing much changes from day to day. Urban life, on the other hand, draws in people who thrive on novelty, ambition, and possibilities — the kind of people who'd feel claustrophobic in a village where everyone knows your business. I don't think one is superior; it's more about what you're wired for. Having said that, I think a lot of people — myself included — end up somewhere in the middle. We live in cities but we romanticise the countryside. Whether we'd actually last a week there is another question entirely.

**Q:** Why do you think the younger generation tends to leave rural areas?
**A:** I think it's pretty straightforward, really — they want more. More opportunities, more choices, more freedom to define who they are. In a small village, your path is kind of laid out for you. Everyone knows your family, there are certain expectations, and the range of careers or lifestyles available is limited. Cities offer this promise of reinvention — you can be whoever you want, try different things, fail and start over. I can relate to that personally. I left for similar reasons. The trade-off is that you lose that sense of rootedness, that connection to a specific place and community. But for most young people, that's a price they're willing to pay, at least for a while.

**Q:** Do you think the traditional rural way of life will disappear eventually?
**A:** It's a difficult question, but if I'm being honest, I think a lot of it will fade away, yes. My generation, for the most part, isn't going back to the village to take over the family farm. We've been educated, we've tasted city life, we've built careers that don't involve working the land. The older generation are still holding on, but when they're gone, who's going to wake up at four in the morning to tend those fields? In a generation or two, villages like mine will probably look very different — they might become tourist spots, or weekend retreats for city people, or they might just empty out entirely. I have mixed feelings about it, to be honest. On one hand, you shouldn't romanticise poverty or backbreaking manual labour — there's a reason people leave. But on the other hand, there's a kind of wisdom and resilience embedded in that way of life — an understanding of the land, of community, of what it means to stay in one place your whole life — that will be lost. And I'm not sure we've really thought through what we're losing.
